Data Visualisation 2 · Australian Museum Collections

Drawers of a Continent

Australia's eight state and territory museums hold more than 64 million objects and specimens — beetles pinned in 1890, shells dredged off the reef, fossils, stone tools, family heirlooms. What exactly is in those drawers, where did it come from, and how much of it can you actually see?

No. 01 · The vaults

Eight vaults, two centuries

Every state and territory runs its own museum. The oldest, the Australian Museum in Sydney, opened in 1827, only 39 years after the First Fleet. The youngest, the Museum and Art Gallery of the Northern Territory, opened in Darwin in 1981.

Size and age don't line up neatly. Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane each look after about 15 million items or more, three times anything held elsewhere. Hobart's museum is the second oldest, yet it holds about 800,000.

Circle area shows the published collection size. Darwin (dashed circle) doesn't publish a single total. Hover any circle for details.

No. 02 · Inside a vault

Mostly small, mostly alive (once)

Open the Australian Museum's stores and you mostly find tiny animals. Insects and molluscs make up about 4 million items each. Marine invertebrates and fish add another 1.6 million each. The cultural collections are smaller in number but no less important: around 800,000 First Nations archaeological artefacts, and about 105,000 First Nations, Pasifika and world-culture objects.

No. 03 · Specialties

Every museum has a personality

Natural history specimens have been digitised much faster than cultural objects, and they're shared on one national platform, the Atlas of Living Australia (ALA). That means we can compare museums like for like. Each row below splits a museum's online records into groups of animals and plants. The darker a cell, the bigger that group's share.

  • Hobart is half botany. TMAG runs the Tasmanian Herbarium, so 51% of its records are plants and fungi.
  • Perth and Adelaide are reptile country. About one in five records at the WA and SA museums is a reptile. WA also leads on spiders and other arthropods (23%).
  • Melbourne is an insect museum. 41% of Museums Victoria's records are insects.
  • A gap, not a fact. The WA Museum lists only 51 insects online. Its insect collection simply hasn't been shared yet.

When a scientist names a new species, one specimen, the holotype, becomes its permanent reference. Disputes about what a name means are settled by going back to that one animal in its drawer. Queensland and Sydney each hold about 60,000 type specimens, and Sydney alone holds over 11,000 holotypes.

No. 04 · Field trips

Collected close to home

Pick a museum. The map shows where its specimens were collected, out of all records with a known Australian state.

Most museums collect in their own backyard. Launceston, Perth, Hobart and Brisbane get 86–91% of their located specimens from their home state. Sydney and Melbourne are the national collectors: almost half of their specimens come from other states or overseas. Queensland is Sydney's biggest "away" ground.

No. 05 · Every square a place

A map drawn by collectors

Split Australia into squares about 100 km wide and count the specimens collected in each. The left map shows the national picture of collecting effort: dense around capital cities, the Great Barrier Reef and Tasmania, and thin across the arid interior. A single square containing Sydney holds 233,425 records, more than the whole online collection of Launceston's museum.

The right map colours each square by the museum that holds the most specimens from it. The borders between museum "territories" closely follow state borders. Click a museum in the legend to see its territory.

The maps show the 4.9 million records whose coordinates fall inside the mapped area. Squares with fewer than 10 records are left out.

No. 06 · The collecting boom

Most of it was collected in living memory

Specimens from the 1800s are prized, but they're rare. The stream below shows when each dated record was collected. Collecting stayed modest until the 1950s, then took off as governments funded biological surveys of the reef, the outback and new national parks. The peak came in the 1990s, with 815,000 records in a single decade. Collecting has slowed everywhere since then. So far in the 2020s, Hobart's museum has added more dated specimens than any other.

For nine decades, Sydney and Melbourne swapped first and second place. Then the order shuffled: Queensland took the lead in the 2000s, and in the 2010s Tasmania and Western Australia climbed to second and third, overtaking much larger museums.

No. 07 · From drawer to database

Most of the vault is still dark

For every 100 objects in the big museums' stores, fewer than ten have a searchable specimen record online. Hobart is the outlier: its herbarium sheets were digitised early, so there are 71 online records for every 100 items it holds.

A record is a start, but it's only a line of text. Only 400,000 of the 6.1 million online records (6.6%) include a photograph. The Australian Museum and Launceston's QVMAG are far ahead, with photos on about one record in seven. Three museums have almost no photos online.

Australia's natural heritage is well looked after in physical drawers. Opening those drawers to everyone — online, with pictures — is the job of the next decade.

"Groups" combine ALA classes and phyla (for example, "Fish" = ray-finned fish + sharks and rays + lungfish). Founding years use each museum's own history: TMAG dates from the Royal Society of Tasmania (1843), and QVMAG opened in 1891.
